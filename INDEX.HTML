Anatomical Regions

Femur Anatomy

🔝

Upper End (Proximal)

  • Head — 2/3rd sphere, covered with cartilage (except fovea capitis)
  • Neck — connects head to shaft, angle of inclination ~126°
  • Greater trochanter — large lateral projection, muscle attachment
  • Lesser trochanter — medial, posteromedial projection below neck
  • Intertrochanteric line (anterior) and crest (posterior)
📏

Shaft (Diaphysis)

  • Cylindrical, slightly bowed anteriorly
  • Linea aspera — posterior roughened ridge for muscle attachment
  • Two lips: medial & lateral lip of linea aspera
  • Nutrient foramen — midshaft, directed upward (faces head)
  • Popliteal surface — smooth triangular area posteriorly, distal shaft
🔽

Lower End (Distal)

  • Medial condyle — projects more inferiorly (weight bearing axis)
  • Lateral condyle — articulates with lateral tibial condyle
  • Intercondylar notch/fossa — attachment for cruciate ligaments
  • Medial & lateral epicondyles — ligament attachments
  • Adductor tubercle — on medial epicondyle for adductor magnus
Muscle Attachments

Who Pulls the Femur?

Gluteus Medius & Minimus
→ Greater Trochanter
Abduction of thigh + medial rotation. Keeps pelvis level during walking (Trendelenburg if paralyzed).
Iliopsoas
→ Lesser Trochanter
Strongest flexor of hip joint. Also lateral rotator. Critical for walking & climbing stairs.
Adductors (Longus, Brevis, Magnus)
→ Linea Aspera (medial lip)
Adduction of thigh. Adductor magnus also extends hip. Key in groin injuries.
Quadriceps Femoris
→ Quadriceps tendon → Patella → Tibial tuberosity
Extends knee joint. Most powerful muscle group. Important for climbing & jumping.
Hamstrings
→ Linea aspera (lateral lip)
Flexion of knee + extension of hip. Biceps femoris also laterally rotates leg. Commonly strained in athletes.
Piriformis
→ Greater Trochanter (apex)
External rotator of thigh. Landmark for sciatic nerve. Piriformis syndrome can mimic sciatica.
Gluteus Maximus
→ Gluteal tuberosity + IT band
Largest muscle in body. Powerful extensor and lateral rotator. Critical for climbing stairs.
Pectineus
→ Pectineal line (below lesser trochanter)
Flexion + adduction of hip. Part of medial compartment. Supplied by femoral nerve.
Vascular Anatomy

Blood Supply

MAIN SUPPLY
Medial Circumflex Femoral Artery

Branch of profunda femoris (or femoral artery). Main supply to femoral head & neck via retinacular arteries. Most important — injury here → avascular necrosis.

SECONDARY
Lateral Circumflex Femoral Artery

Also from profunda femoris. Supplies greater trochanter, anterior femoral neck, and lateral head region. Less critical than medial.

RETINACULAR
Retinacular Arteries

Travel along femoral neck under synovial membrane. Main supply to the femoral head in adults. These are the arteries most at risk in neck of femur fractures.

MINOR
Artery of Ligamentum Teres

Branch of obturator artery. Supplies small area around fovea capitis. Functionally important only in children — not sufficient in adults to prevent AVN.

⚠️

CLINICAL PEARL — Avascular Necrosis (AVN)

Disruption of the medial circumflex femoral artery (especially its retinacular branches) in a neck of femur fracture → femoral head loses its blood supply → AVN. This is the most feared complication of intracapsular fractures in the elderly. The femoral head literally "dies" without blood — leading to collapse and severe arthritis.

Trauma & Orthopedics

Femur Fractures

01

Neck of Femur Fracture

Cause

Low-energy fall in elderly (osteoporotic bone). High-energy trauma in young adults. Intracapsular = within joint capsule.

Symptoms

Hip pain, inability to weight bear. Limb shortened + externally rotated. Groin tenderness on palpation.

Complications

AVN of femoral head (#1 feared). Non-union. Osteoarthritis. Deep vein thrombosis.

Treatment

Undisplaced: internal fixation (screws). Displaced in elderly: hemiarthroplasty or THA. Young patients: urgent ORIF.

⚠️ Blood supply at major risk — retinacular arteries torn
02

Intertrochanteric Fracture

Cause

Fall in elderly. Extracapsular fracture — outside joint capsule, between greater and lesser trochanters.

Symptoms

Hip pain + limb shortening. External rotation (iliopsoas pulls lesser trochanter). Ecchymosis may appear later.

Complications

Better blood supply here → AVN less common. Risk of malunion, varus deformity, implant failure.

Treatment

Dynamic Hip Screw (DHS) is gold standard. Intramedullary nail for unstable patterns. Early mobilization key.

✓ Better prognosis — extracapsular, blood supply intact
03

Shaft Fracture

Cause

High-energy trauma: RTA, fall from height, gunshot. Can lose 1–1.5 liters of blood into thigh! Life-threatening.

Symptoms

Severe thigh pain, deformity, swelling. Thigh may be markedly swollen due to haematoma. Unable to move.

Complications

Hypovolaemic shock (blood loss). Fat embolism. Malunion, leg length discrepancy. Injury to femoral vessels.

Treatment

Intramedullary nail (IMN) — gold standard. Resuscitation first. External fixator for open fractures initially.

⚠️ Up to 1.5L blood loss — treat as major trauma
Pathology

Femur Diseases

🦴

Osteoporosis

HIGH YIELD

Reduced bone density → fragility fractures. Femoral neck most vulnerable. Post-menopausal women at highest risk. Diagnosed by DEXA scan (T-score < −2.5). Treat with bisphosphonates, Ca²⁺, Vit D.

💀

Avascular Necrosis (AVN)

HIGH YIELD

Death of femoral head due to lost blood supply. Causes: trauma, steroids, alcohol, sickle cell disease, SLE. X-ray shows "crescent sign". Late: collapse → arthritis. Treatment: core decompression (early) or THA (late).

🦠

Osteomyelitis

IMPORTANT

Bone infection — most common causative organism: S. aureus. Haematogenous spread in children (metaphysis of femur). Presents with fever, bone pain, raised ESR/CRP. CT/MRI confirms. Treated with IV antibiotics ± surgical debridement.

🔬

Bone Tumors

HIGH YIELD

Osteosarcoma — most common primary malignant bone tumor, distal femur metaphysis, peak in teens. "Sunburst pattern" + Codman's triangle on X-ray. Ewing's sarcoma — diaphysis, "onion skin" periosteal reaction. Both require chemotherapy + surgical resection.

Did You Know?

Femur Fun Facts

30×
Femur can withstand up to 30 times your body weight — more than any other bone in the body!
48cm
The average adult femur is ~48cm long — it is the longest and heaviest bone in the human body.
⚡
Break this bone = serious trauma. Femur shaft fractures can cause 1–1.5L of blood loss into the thigh alone — a life-threatening emergency.
🦴
The femur is the only bone in the thigh. It articulates with 3 bones: the hip bone, tibia, and patella.
